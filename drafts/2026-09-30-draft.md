# Stuck in the Hospital: A Market Test for Hawaiʻi’s Nursing-Home Waitlist

## The Challenge

Every year, thousands of Hawaiʻi patients finish hospital treatment but cannot leave, because no nursing home[^1] or other care setting can take them yet. In 2024, 3,123 of these “waitlisted” patients spent 60,876 days in acute-care beds, an average of 19.5 days each (State Health Planning and Development Agency [SHPDA], 2025b). My benchmark is a 14-day average, looser than Scotland’s two-week maximum for every patient (NHS Borders, 2015) because public data give only totals. Hawaiʻi met it in 2017, 2018, 2020 and 2021 but not since, peaking at 23.0 days in 2023 (SHPDA, n.d.; Figure 1). It matters now because the relief that followed Medicaid’s 2024 rate reset may not last.

Waiting in hospital is “a poor outcome for the individual” (NHS Borders, 2015, p. 1), and it costs hospitals. Against the benchmark, 2024 had at least 17,154 excess days, about 47 beds on an average day. Medicaid pays a hospital $486.76 for a waitlisted day (Med-QUEST, 2025). I assume the hospital’s avoidable cost[^2] is $550 to $1,470, so each excess day loses it $63 to $983, or $1.1 million to $16.9 million a year (Appendix A). This is an externality: a home that declines a patient leaves a cost with the hospital that the home does not bear. The Healthcare Association of Hawaii, which represents the hospitals, has four options: wait for Medicaid’s 2024 rate reset to work; lobby for a Medicaid add-on for hard-to-place patients; have hospitals pay homes a per-day top-up; or build new transitional capacity[^3] through a partnership.

## The Economics and Three Tests

Medicaid pays nursing homes an administered price: the state sets the daily rate, and homes are price takers.[^4] For years it worked like a price ceiling below cost: the median rate sat 19 to 29% below the median cost of homes serving mostly Medicaid patients (Figure 2). My hypothesis was opportunity cost. A Medicaid resident stays about 543 days at the median Hawaiʻi home (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], n.d.), while the same bed could serve a run of short, better-paid Medicare stays. Counting that forgone margin as a cost, as economic profit[^5] does, a home can lose on a Medicaid patient even when the rate covers the cost of care. If so, admissions should be elastic[^6] to the Medicaid rate: raising it relative to other payers should shrink the group waiting for “financial” reasons.

Hawaiʻi raised it twice. The median rate rose about 18% by July 2021, after a 12% adjustment for private homes in January 2021, and 30% at the January 2024 reset (Med-QUEST, n.d., 2024), while Medicare’s nursing-home payment rose 1.2% and 4.2% (CMS, 2021, 2024). I set three tests on September 28, after seeing the 2023 and 2024 data but before pulling 2017 to 2022, and fixed their fine print after seeing the results (Appendix B). Test 1: the hypothesis fails unless the financial share of the December 31 waitlist falls at least 5 points after an increase. Test 2: waiting wins if days per patient were already trending toward 14. Test 3: capacity wins if “no bed available” is the largest reason in most years.

Test 1 came back falsified. The financial share rose 1.9 points from 2020 to 2021 and 11.1 points from 2023 to 2024, to 35% (Figure 3). A rise is not a weak, inelastic response; something else moved. The category is undefined and mixes home refusals with pending eligibility decisions, the count is a one-day snapshot of fewer than 200 patients, and COVID clouds 2021. Read narrowly, two unconditional increases did not shrink the group, reason not to bet public money on an add-on.

Test 3 passed, but on the wrong years. “No bed” led from 2017 through 2021, when the benchmark was met in four years of five. It has not led since, and its share fell from 39% in 2019 to 18% in 2024 while the financial share climbed from 17% to 35%. Figure 3 does not show a bed shortage today; neither price nor beds is proven to be the binding limit.

Test 2 was not met: days per patient had drifted up nearly two days a year (Figure 1). That test stops at 2023, and 2024 is waiting’s best evidence: waitlist days fell 27%. But the reset has no scheduled rebase, only an index update (Med-QUEST, 2024), and the median rate went from $481.83 in January 2024 to $509.69 in January 2025, then down to $495.26 in January 2026, an unexplained drop (Med-QUEST, n.d., 2025), a net 1.4% a year. If costs grow at Medicare’s market-basket pace, 3.0 to 3.3% (CMS, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026), the rate falls 2.1% below Medicaid-heavy homes’ cost in fiscal 2026 and 4.0% in 2027, reopening the gap.

## Recommendation

The data cannot say whether the limit is willingness or beds, so the Association should make homes reveal it. Its hospitals should offer a per-day payment, on top of Medicaid’s rate, to any home that guarantees to admit waitlisted patients. The ceiling is what a hospital saves: $63 to $983 a day for the 19.5 days of an average wait, or $1,233 to $19,166 per placed patient, each hospital setting its own amount. The low end rests on my 15% avoidable-cost assumption. The rule: if within six months homes commit existing staffed beds equal to the 2024 gap, about 47, the top-up works and should continue. If they do not, even at offers near the ceiling, the limit is beds and staff, and the Association should pursue transitional capacity through a partnership. I chose these terms on September 30, after the results; six months is a judgment, matching the twice-yearly rate cycle, and 47 beds assumes full occupancy and covers all levels of care.

I expect the capacity branch: 353 long-term-care beds sit idle for lack of staff (SHPDA, 2025a), so homes may have little to commit. That is an expectation, not a finding. Who should pay, hospitals or Medicaid, is a normative question; the test answers the positive one.

The strongest objection is that I have just shown price does not work, and now propose paying homes more. But test 1 measured unconditional increases: more for every Medicaid day in every home, whether or not the home took one waitlisted patient. Much of that money was economic rent[^7] on beds already filled. The offer pays only at the margin, for a guaranteed admission. If the objection is right anyway, homes will not commit, hospitals pay nothing, and the answer points the Association to capacity. That branch has its own obstacle, which I do not cost here: new beds need the same scarce workers.

In short, the Association should neither wait nor lobby for an unproven add-on. It should offer homes a capped, conditional payment for six months, keep it if about 47 beds are committed, and build transitional capacity if not. The limits are the one-day, self-reported reason counts, the unknown payer mix, and my avoidable-cost assumption.

[^1]: A nursing home (skilled nursing facility, SNF) gives 24-hour care to people who no longer need a hospital but cannot safely go home. Medicare (people 65 and older) pays for short rehabilitation stays; Medicaid (people with low incomes) pays for most long stays.

[^2]: Avoidable cost is what a hospital stops spending when a patient leaves a day sooner. I assume 15 to 40% of the $3,664 average expense per adjusted inpatient day at Hawaiʻi nonprofit hospitals in 2023 (KFF, n.d.), since much of that average is overhead.

[^3]: A transitional bed is a short-stay nursing bed, used for weeks rather than years, for patients leaving the hospital while a longer-term placement is arranged.

[^4]: An administered price is set by an authority, not by supply and demand. A price taker cannot change the price it gets. A price ceiling is a legal maximum; set below cost, it causes a shortage.

[^5]: Economic profit is revenue minus all costs, including opportunity cost: what a resource would earn in its best alternative use.

[^6]: Elasticity is how strongly quantity responds to price; inelastic means it responds little.

[^7]: Economic rent is payment above what is needed to keep a resource in its current use.
