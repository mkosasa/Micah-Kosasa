# Perfect Competition — Brief

## Purpose
Ground the economic concept of perfect competition before applying it to any analysis of the healthcare markets Queen's Health Systems operates in. This brief defines the model, its assumptions, and where real-world healthcare markets diverge from it.

## Definition
Perfect competition is a market structure in which no single buyer or seller can influence the market price. Price is set entirely by aggregate supply and demand, and every firm is a "price taker."

## Core Assumptions
- **Many buyers and sellers** — no single participant has enough market share to affect price.
- **Homogeneous product** — goods are perfect substitutes; buyers have no reason to prefer one seller over another.
- **Free entry and exit** — firms can enter or leave the market without significant barriers or sunk costs.
- **Perfect information** — buyers and sellers have full knowledge of prices, quality, and available alternatives.
- **No externalities** — the full cost and benefit of a transaction are captured by the buyer and seller.

## Firm Behavior
- Firms produce where marginal cost equals price (P = MC), since they cannot set price independently.
- In the long run, economic profit trends to zero as free entry attracts new competitors until price falls to the level of average total cost.
- Output and pricing decisions are the only real levers a firm has; there is no benefit to marketing, differentiation, or negotiation.

## Why This Model Matters as a Baseline
Perfect competition is rarely observed in practice, but it serves as the theoretical benchmark for market efficiency: it produces the allocatively and productively efficient outcome (price equals marginal cost, output where marginal cost equals marginal benefit). Every other market structure — monopolistic competition, oligopoly, monopoly — is best understood as a deviation from this baseline, and the size of that deviation is a rough proxy for market power and potential inefficiency.

## Where Healthcare Diverges
Hospital and health system markets, including Queen's, violate nearly every assumption above:
- **Concentrated supply.** In Hawai'i, a small number of health systems serve the state, giving individual systems meaningful pricing influence in local markets — the opposite of the "many sellers" assumption.
- **Differentiated, not homogeneous, service.** Facilities, specialists, network affiliations, and quality reputations differ meaningfully between providers.
- **High barriers to entry.** Capital requirements, licensure, certificate-of-need regulation, and physician network access make entry difficult.
- **Severe information asymmetry.** Patients rarely have full visibility into price or quality before receiving care, and third-party payers (insurers, Medicare, Medicaid) intermediate the transaction.
- **Significant externalities and regulation.** Public health outcomes, uncompensated care, and regulatory compliance requirements mean price and quantity are shaped by more than supply and demand alone.

## Implications for Compliance and Strategy Work
Because healthcare markets are structurally imperfect, competitive and regulatory scrutiny (antitrust review, rate transparency rules, network adequacy requirements) exists specifically to counteract the market power that would not arise under perfect competition. Any capability or initiative that touches pricing, network contracting, or market positioning should be evaluated against this gap — the further a market is from the perfect-competition baseline, the more regulatory and reputational exposure a pricing or contracting decision carries.

## Open Questions for Follow-On Work
- Which of Queen's service lines face the most competitive (closest to perfectly competitive) local markets, versus the most concentrated?
- Where does regulatory reporting already require documenting market concentration (e.g., antitrust, CON filings), and can that data be reused for internal strategy analysis?
