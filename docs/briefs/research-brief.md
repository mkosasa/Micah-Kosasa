# Research paper: engagement brief

## The problem

I am deciding, as a policy advisor to the State of Hawaii (the Legislature and Med-QUEST), how many new short-stay transitional nursing-home beds the state should fund in partnership with hospitals, as an alternative to raising Medicaid nursing-facility rates statewide far enough to draw in new capacity.

What is fixed (from the data gathered so far): the 2023 acute-care waitlist (3,610 patients and 83,097 waitlist days, about 23 days each, holding about 228 acute beds on an average day); Medicaid's January 2026 rates ($495 median nursing-facility day, $486.76 per waitlisted hospital day); the rate method in place since January 2024 (prices reset to 2023 costs, then moved only by a national index, with no scheduled rebase); FY2023 nursing-home cost (median $462 a day for Medicaid-heavy homes); and the price of the alternative (each +$10/day statewide rate increase costs about $5.3M a year before the federal match, across 526,866 Medicaid days). Medicaid sets the price, and nursing homes and hospitals take it as given for Medicaid patients.

What I'm choosing is the number of transitional beds (about 30-day stays) the state funds with hospitals. The capital cost is whatever it takes to put a bed into service: converting, buying or leasing existing space (an unused hospital wing, a closed facility, licensed beds standing empty) or, where no such space exists, a new building. That capital cost, net of what the rate's capital component already covers, is split between the state and hospitals in proportion to what each side saves. The beds are paired with a statewide rate rule (a periodic rebase or a Hawaii-specific index applied to every nursing home's Medicaid rate, not just the new beds), subject to each new bed's operating cost excluding capital staying at or below the Medicaid rate excluding its capital component.

If I get the number wrong, I either leave hospitals holding patients they can't discharge (displacing acute patients and building pressure for a statewide rate increase), or commit state and hospital money to beds that sit empty or can't take the patients who are actually stuck.

## What I am assuming

- The waitlist, cost-report and rate figures above describe the situation as it stands and are fixed for this decision (several rate medians were read by OCR and still need spot checks).
- The Medicaid rate works as a price ceiling: nursing homes take it as given and limit Medicaid admissions when it falls below cost.
- Nursing homes line up on a cost ladder, and a home takes Medicaid patients only when the rate covers its average variable cost.
- A transitional bed runs at 90% occupancy with ~30-day stays, and each admission avoids the 2023 average of ~23 hospital waitlist days, freeing about 252 hospital days per bed-year (0.90 × 365 ÷ 30 × 23).
- The Dec 31, 2023 mix of waitlist reasons holds for the whole year, and the 45 financial/Medicaid/insurance cases on that day were mostly nursing homes declining Medicaid patients rather than pending eligibility decisions.
- Capital does not have to mean new construction. The first beds go into the cheapest existing space that can be converted, and a new building is the high-cost case, not the default. Licensed nursing-home beds often run 50 to 75% occupied, but some of that may reflect staffing shortages rather than a lack of space.
- The new beds are paid the same Medicaid rate as every other home. The capital component already built into that rate counts toward the beds' capital cost, and the partnership funds only the remaining gap.
- Without a state commitment, each hospital would rather another hospital paid for the beds.
- Annualized capital cost is the present value of the capital spread over the life of the space at a long-term borrowing rate, and the beds can be added in phases rather than all at once.
- I treat the state as one market, although in practice the beds would have to be where the waitlisted patients are.

The one I'd most want to test if I had more time: the hospital's avoidable cost of a waitlist day, meaning what a hospital actually stops spending when a waitlisted patient leaves a day sooner. It is the largest input to the savings and the least directly observable. The only published figure, about $3,664 per adjusted inpatient day (Hawaii nonprofit hospitals, 2023), is an average that includes overhead that doesn't go away when one low-acuity patient leaves.

For a Medicaid patient, Medicaid pays roughly $490 a day in either setting ($486.76 to the hospital for a waitlisted day, $495 median to a nursing home), so what the system saves on each freed day is the avoidable cost above that. At about 252 freed days per bed-year, every $100 of avoidable cost above $490 is worth about $25,200 per bed per year. Plugging in the $3,664 average would put each bed's saving near $800,000 a year and make almost any bed count look worthwhile, which is why the average can't stand in for the avoidable cost. If the true figure sits only modestly above $490, even the first beds may not cover their annualized capital cost. This one input decides both whether any beds pay and how far past the easy cases the marginal bed can still pay, and the $3,664 headline can't answer that.

The shortage itself comes from the supply ladder. Line Hawaii's nursing homes up from lowest to highest running cost, and each home supplies Medicaid beds only when the rate covers its average variable cost. The supply curve is that ladder above the floor, with nothing below it. The rate is priced near the median cost, so homes with above-median costs start at or below the line, and any drift between Hawaii costs and the national index pushes more homes under it each year. Homes below the line don't close; they ration, keeping Medicaid admissions down even when they have the space and the patient needs the bed. That is how I read the 45 financial/Medicaid waitlist cases.

The bed count itself turns on how fast the marginal bed runs out of patients it can take. A transitional bed frees its ~252 days only while there are waitlisted patients an ordinary nursing-home bed can accept. On Dec 31, 2023, 42 of 191 waitlisted patients were waiting because no bed was available and 45 for financial, Medicaid or insurance reasons; I read most of the second group as nursing homes turning away Medicaid patients (the shortage a price ceiling predicts), which beds that take Medicaid would clear. Together that is up to 87 of 191 (46%), or about 37,900 of 2023's 83,097 waitlist days: about 150 beds' worth. Most of the other 104 are behavioral or dementia (48), family or guardianship (28), or special-care (26) cases that need specialized staffing or a legal decision, not just an empty bed. Past ~150 beds, the marginal bed's freed days drop sharply while its annualized capital and operating cost stays the same or rises: the cheapest convertible space gets used first, and once it runs out, the next beds need a new building. That is the same ladder on the capital side, with the cheapest space on the bottom rung. A falling marginal saving against a flat-to-rising marginal cost is the mechanical reason the count stops where it does, not simply that "more beds, more relief" runs out.

On the capital side, Medicaid already pays for buildings. Hawaii's nursing-facility rate is built from components (direct care, administration and general, and capital), each priced since January 2024 at the day-weighted median of 2023 costs. The capital component covers depreciation, interest, rent and property costs, and it is paid on every occupied day. The new beds get the same rate as every other home, and that capital component goes toward their capital cost, so the partnership pays only the gap between the beds' annualized capital cost and what the component brings in. That keeps one rate statewide and keeps Medicaid from paying for the same space twice: once through a grant and again through the per diem. The size of the gap depends on the space. For converted existing space, the component may cover most or all of the capital cost and the grant shrinks toward zero. For a new building, the component is set at the median capital cost of existing homes, many of them older buildings with much of their cost already depreciated, so it covers only part, and the grant covers the rest.

On the operating side, the capital is then fully paid for, so the new beds only have to cover their operating cost excluding capital with the rate excluding its capital component. That is the shutdown test (P ≥ AVC) rather than full cost (ATC), with capital taken out of both sides so the comparison is like for like. That test has to hold for the life of the space, and the current rate method won't keep it there by itself. At July 2023 rates, 17 of 18 Medicaid-heavy homes were below cost (median $462 cost vs. $371 rate). The January 2024 reset closed that gap for now, but rates now move only with a national index, while Hawaii's median cost grew about 4.0% a year from FY2011 to FY2023 (6.4% at the median from FY2022 to FY2023). If Hawaii costs outrun the index by 1.5 points a year, rates end up about 14% below cost after ten years. The drift hits every Medicaid nursing home, not just the new beds, so the rule applies statewide. Without it, the operating loss on each occupied day grows every year, and over the life of the space it turns a one-time capital investment into a permanent subsidy. That is why the rule is part of the recommendation, not an add-on.

The state's own budget barely moves per patient, since Medicaid pays about the same per day in either setting; the case rests on system-wide savings: hospitals' avoided costs, acute patients who get the freed bed (worth extra margin only when a hospital is full), and the rate increase the state avoids. Those savings are a positive externality: a bed one hospital pays for also clears waitlists at other hospitals and serves acute patients it never sees, so no single funder captures the full benefit, and the market builds too few beds. A policy works best when it is aimed as close to the root cause of a distortion as possible, rather than spread across the whole market. The root causes here are missing capacity for Medicaid patients and a rate that drifts. A statewide rate increase is the untargeted fix: it pays on all 526,866 Medicaid days, including beds whose capital is already sunk. Funding capital only for the new beds, and only for the part the rate's capital component doesn't already cover, targets the capacity problem, and the rate rule targets the drift. Splitting that cost in proportion to benefit decides who pays each share, but not how many beds are worth adding: the count is set by system-wide saving against cost, whoever holds the bill.

Who pays is where the partnership can stall. Among hospitals, funding beds is a prisoner's dilemma. Every hospital gains when beds get built, because patients from every hospital use them, but each gains more if another hospital pays. As long as one hospital's own share of the saving is less than the cost of a bed, holding back is its better move whatever the others do, so the Nash equilibrium is that nobody funds and too few beds get built. That is the positive externality in game form. The partnership turns this into a sequential game. The state moves first by committing its capital share and the rate rule, and each hospital then decides whether to join. Working the tree backward, a hospital joins when its share of the cost, set in proportion to its benefit, is less than its saving, which a benefit-based split is designed to guarantee. So the state's commitment has to come first: an outside commitment changes each hospital's payoffs and moves them out of an equilibrium none of them can leave on their own.

Time enters in two places. First, the annualized capital cost is the present value of the capital spread over the life of the space at the funder's borrowing rate, so the discount rate matters. Rising interest rates raise the annual cost of capital and pull the bed count down at the margin. But space financed over decades is priced off long-term rates, which do not always move with short-term rates. I'll run the model at more than one rate. Second, the beds don't have to be committed all at once. Starting with converted existing space and keeping the option to expand toward 150 only if the waitlist falls as predicted is a real option. It is worth the most exactly when the key input, avoidable cost, is uncertain, and it limits the loss if I'm wrong.

## Hypothesis

I expect about 150 transitional beds to be the number worth funding, but I hold this loosely because it is not yet backed by the model. The reasoning is that each bed frees about 252 hospital days a year only while it can take patients stuck for lack of a bed that accepts Medicaid. On Dec 31, 2023, that meant the 42 waiting because no bed was available plus most of the 45 held up on financial/Medicaid grounds, up to 46% of the waitlist or roughly 37,900 of 2023's 83,097 waitlist days. Past that, the marginal bed mostly meets patients held by behavioral needs, special care or guardianship, which an ordinary transitional bed can't take, so its freed days fall off while its capital and operating cost doesn't fall, and rises once convertible existing space runs out and the next beds need a new building. That is why I don't expect the answer to approach the ~330 beds that would absorb every waitlist day. I expect the first beds to pay off because a waitlist day's avoidable cost should sit well above the ~$490 Medicaid pays in either setting, but that is the input I'm least sure of, and if it is close to $490 the answer could be zero. And I expect the partnership to hold up only with the rate rule, since otherwise the beds' operating loss grows each year as Hawaii costs outrun the national index. I also don't expect hospitals to fund the beds on their own: each gains more if another hospital pays, so without the state moving first, I expect too few beds to be built. This guess is not derived from the model. It's my starting intuition ahead of building it, and the actual crossover point is what the model needs to determine.

## How I would know I was wrong

If the model put the number of beds worth funding well below 125, near the ~75 that the "no bed available" cases alone would fill, that would mean most of the financial/Medicaid/insurance group is waiting on eligibility decisions, not on nursing homes that won't take Medicaid. The bottom of my band needs about two-thirds of that group (about 30 of 45) to be clearable by a bed that accepts Medicaid. A result below it would falsify the price-ceiling reading the 150 depends on, and would point the recommendation toward faster eligibility decisions rather than more beds.

If the model showed beds still paying off well past 175, toward the ~330 it would take to absorb every waitlist day, the marginal bed doesn't run out of patients where I've argued: either the behavioral, special-care and guardianship cases move once beds exist, or the Dec 31 snapshot understates the plain bed shortage. That would undercut the mechanism that caps the count, not just the headline number.

If, across a reasonable range of avoidable costs, even the first beds, placed in the cheapest convertible existing space, didn't cover their annualized capital cost plus any operating loss, the partnership fails at any size: the hospital's avoidable cost of a waitlist day would be too close to the ~$490 Medicaid already pays, and capacity isn't the problem to solve.

If the model showed that one hospital's own savings from the first beds cover their full cost, so that a large system would fund them without anyone else, the prisoner's dilemma isn't what holds building back. The state wouldn't need to move first, and the case for public capital would shrink to the rate rule alone. The same holds if hospitals still wouldn't join after the state commits, with each hospital's share set by its benefit: the sequential game would not play out the way I've argued, and something other than free riding, such as staffing or regulation, is what stops the beds.

If the new beds' operating cost excluding capital came out above the Medicaid rate excluding its capital component, even with the rate rule in place, that would mean I misunderstood the operating side, independent of the bed-count argument, and the partnership would be a continuing subsidy, not a one-time investment.

I would not consider the hypothesis wrong if the model landed between 125 and 175 beds, within 25 of my guess. That band stays clear of the ~75 and ~330 cases, so a result inside it would confirm that the bed-shortage and Medicaid-refusal cases are what sets the size, while letting the model pin down the exact crossover more precisely than my estimate could.
