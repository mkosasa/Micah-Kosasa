# Research paper — engagement brief

## The problem

I am deciding, as a policy advisor to the State of Hawaii (the Legislature and Med-QUEST), how many new short-stay transitional nursing-home beds the state should back through a partnership with hospitals, as an alternative to raising Medicaid nursing-facility rates statewide far enough to draw in new capacity.

The problem falls on patients first. In 2023, 3,610 people who no longer needed acute care waited an average of about 23 days in hospital beds for a place to go, and their families waited with them. It also falls on the acute patients who can't get those beds, on hospitals that carry days the payment doesn't cover, and on the payers and taxpayers behind them. It matters now because the relief from the January 2024 rate reset is built to fade. The reset raised the median Medicaid rate about 30%, and waitlist days fell 27% in 2024, but rates now move only with a national index, so the gap reopens as Hawaii's costs outrun it, while an aging population keeps adding demand.

What is fixed (from the data gathered so far): the 2023 acute-care waitlist, taken the day before the reset, as my baseline (3,610 patients and 83,097 waitlist days, about 228 acute beds held on an average day), with 2024 (60,876 waitlist days) as the after-reset comparison; Medicaid's January 2026 rates ($495 median nursing-facility day, $486.76 per waitlisted hospital day); the rate method in place since January 2024 (prices reset to 2023 costs, then moved only by a national index, with no scheduled rebase); FY2023 nursing-home cost (median $462 a day for Medicaid-heavy homes); and the price of a statewide increase (about $5.3M a year for every $10 a day, before the federal match, across 526,866 Medicaid days). Medicaid sets the price, and nursing homes and hospitals take it as given for Medicaid patients.

What I'm choosing is the number of transitional beds (about 30-day stays) to add. The capital cost is whatever it takes to put a bed into service: converting, buying or leasing existing space or, where none exists, a new building. Hospitals fund that capital through a consortium under a binding contract, each paying in proportion to what it saves, net of what the rate's capital component already covers. The state's part is commitment rather than money: it enforces the contract, speeds certificate-of-need approval, and adopts a statewide rate rule (a periodic rebase or a Hawaii-specific index) so the rate keeps covering running costs. The choice is limited by staffing, by where patients can go after their stay, and by the requirement that each new bed's operating cost excluding capital stay at or below the Medicaid rate excluding its capital component.

If I get the number wrong in one direction, each bed short leaves about 252 waitlist days a year in hospitals, worth roughly $139,000 to $370,000 in avoidable hospital cost per bed. In the other, each bed too many costs about $227,000 a year at my break-even estimate while freeing few hospital days, because the patients it could take are already placed.

## What I am assuming

- The waitlist, cost-report and rate figures above describe the situation as it stands and are fixed for this decision (several rate medians were read by OCR and still need spot checks).
- The Medicaid rate works as a price ceiling on a cost ladder: a nursing home takes Medicaid patients only when the rate covers its average variable cost, and limits admissions when it doesn't.
- A transitional bed runs at 90% occupancy with ~30-day stays, and each admission avoids the 2023 average of ~23 hospital waitlist days, freeing about 252 hospital days per bed-year (0.90 × 365 ÷ 30 × 23). After the stay, patients go home, to home care or to a care home; if onward placement stretches stays to 60 or 90 days, a bed frees about 126 or 84 days instead.
- The Dec 31, 2023 mix of waitlist reasons holds for the whole year, and the 45 financial/Medicaid/insurance cases on that day were mostly nursing homes declining Medicaid patients rather than pending eligibility decisions.
- The transitional patients are Medicaid patients, or dual-eligible patients past their Medicare days. Short post-acute stays are often Medicare Part A days, which pay well, so this needs checking.
- Capital does not have to mean new construction. The first beds go into the cheapest existing space that can be converted, and a new building is the high-cost case. Licensed nursing-home beds often run 50 to 75% occupied, but some of that may reflect staffing shortages rather than a lack of space.
- The new beds are paid the same Medicaid rate as every other home, and the capital component built into that rate counts toward their capital cost.
- The bed count is set on a system-wide resource view: Medicaid payments are transfers between parties, so the comparison is avoidable hospital cost against the new bed's full cost. Who pays whom sets only the cost split.
- Without a binding agreement, each hospital would rather another hospital paid for the beds.
- Annualized capital cost is the present value of the capital spread over the life of the space at a long-term borrowing rate, and the beds can be added in phases.
- I treat the state as one market, although in practice the beds would have to be where the waitlisted patients are.

The one I'd most want to test if I had more time: the hospital's avoidable cost of a waitlist day, meaning what a hospital actually stops spending when a waitlisted patient leaves a day sooner. It is the largest input to the savings and the least directly observable. The only published figure, about $3,664 per adjusted inpatient day (Hawaii nonprofit hospitals, 2023), is an average that includes overhead that doesn't go away when one low-acuity patient leaves. I set its range in advance at 15 to 40% of that average, about $550 to $1,470 a day.

At about 252 freed days per bed-year, every $100 of avoidable cost is worth about $25,200 per bed per year. Against that, the bed costs its capital plus the running cost of about 328 occupied days a year. At the FY2023 median cost of $462 a day, the running cost alone comes to about $600 per freed hospital day, and capital adds more: little for converted space, more for a new building. Plugging in the $3,664 average would put each bed's saving near $920,000 a year and make almost any bed count look worthwhile, which is why the average can't stand in for the avoidable cost. This one input decides both whether any beds pay and how far past the easy cases the marginal bed can still pay.

The shortage itself comes from the supply ladder. Line Hawaii's nursing homes up from lowest to highest running cost, and each home supplies Medicaid beds only when the rate covers its average variable cost. The rate is priced near the median cost, so homes with above-median costs start at or below the line, and any drift between Hawaii costs and the national index pushes more homes under it each year. Homes below the line don't close; they ration Medicaid admissions. That is how I read the 45 financial/Medicaid waitlist cases, but the one later snapshot cuts against it: on Dec 31, 2024, after rates rose about 30%, that group grew to 60 while "no bed available" fell from 42 to 31. If the group were mostly refusals, it should have shrunk. One day against one day doesn't settle it, and I keep the reading as my hypothesis, but it is the evidence most likely to prove me wrong.

The bed count itself turns on how fast the marginal bed runs out of patients it can take. A transitional bed frees its ~252 days only while there are waitlisted patients an ordinary nursing-home bed can accept. On Dec 31, 2023, 42 of 191 waitlisted patients were waiting because no bed was available and 45 for financial, Medicaid or insurance reasons. Together that is up to 87 of 191 (46%), or about 37,900 of 2023's 83,097 waitlist days: about 150 beds' worth. Most of the other 104 are behavioral or dementia (48), family or guardianship (28), or special-care (26) cases that need specialized staffing or a legal decision, not just an empty bed. Past ~150 beds, the marginal bed's freed days drop sharply while its cost stays flat or rises, because the cheapest convertible space is used first and later beds need a new building.

## Hypothesis

I expect about 150 transitional beds to be the number worth adding, but I hold this loosely because it is not yet backed by the model. The reasoning is that each bed frees about 252 hospital days a year only while it can take patients stuck for lack of a bed that accepts Medicaid, which on Dec 31, 2023 meant the 42 waiting because no bed was available plus most of the 45 held up on financial/Medicaid grounds. Past that, the marginal bed mostly meets patients held by behavioral needs, special care or guardianship, so its freed days fall off while its cost doesn't fall, and rises once convertible space runs out. That is why I don't expect the answer to approach the ~330 beds that would absorb every waitlist day. I expect the first beds to break even at an avoidable hospital cost of about $900 a day, and the true avoidable cost to sit somewhat above that, near the middle of my $550 to $1,470 range. That margin is thin, and it rests on the input I'm least sure of. I expect the partnership to hold up only with the rate rule, and I don't expect hospitals to fund the beds without a binding agreement, since each gains more if another pays. This guess is my starting intuition ahead of building the model, not a result of it.

## How I would know I was wrong

The test I am most likely to fail is outside the model: the waitlist reasons. The bottom of my band, 125 beds, needs about two-thirds of the financial/Medicaid group (about 30 of 45) to be patients a bed that accepts Medicaid would clear. If the tables for other years show that group holding steady or growing as rates rise, as the 2024 snapshot already does (45 to 60), it is mostly waiting on eligibility decisions. The pool is then closer to the ~75 beds the "no bed available" cases alone would fill, and the recommendation should shift toward faster eligibility decisions rather than more beds.

If other years show a much larger plain bed shortage than Dec 31, 2023, or the behavioral, special-care and guardianship cases move once beds exist, beds could keep paying well past 175, toward the ~330 it would take to absorb every waitlist day. The mechanism that caps the count would then be wrong, not just the headline number.

If the model puts the break-even avoidable cost above $1,470, the top of my range, even the first beds, placed in the cheapest converted space, don't pay, and capacity isn't the problem to solve. If it comes out below $550, every bed in the pool pays easily, and my $900 guess was far off even if the count holds.

If patients can't move on after their stay, the bed stops working as a transitional bed. At about $227,000 a year per bed, if average stays run past about 50 days, each bed frees too few hospital days and the break-even rises above $1,470. The partnership then fails unless care-home or home-care capacity is added with it.

If one hospital's own avoided waitlist days, valued at its avoidable cost, cover the full cost of the beds those days would fill, that hospital would build without partners. The prisoner's dilemma isn't what holds building back, and the consortium and the state's commitment add little beyond the rate rule. The model also computes each hospital's benefit-based share of the capital cost against its own saving; if any hospital's share exceeds its saving, that hospital would not join even with the state enforcing the contract, and the consortium design fails.

If the new beds' operating cost excluding capital came out above the Medicaid rate excluding its capital component, even with the rate rule in place, that would mean I misunderstood the operating side, independent of the bed-count argument, and the partnership would be a continuing subsidy, not a one-time investment.

I would not consider the hypothesis wrong if the model landed between 125 and 175 beds. The lower edge is derived from the waitlist reasons; the upper edge is symmetric by choice, not derived. The band stays clear of the ~75 and ~330 cases, so a result inside it would confirm that the bed-shortage and Medicaid-refusal cases are what sets the size.

## The analysis I plan to run

For each added bed, I will compute the hospital days it frees and their value at avoidable hospital cost, and compare that with the bed's full cost (annualized capital plus the running cost of its occupied days), treating Medicaid payments as transfers and adding beds while the saving covers the cost. The inputs come from the SHPDA waitlist tables, the CMS nursing-home cost reports and the Med-QUEST rate memos, run across avoidable costs of $550 to $1,470, capital cost per bed for converted space and a new building, more than one discount rate, and stays of 30, 60 and 90 days. Alongside the bed count I will test whether the rate excluding its capital component covers the new beds' running cost, now and as costs drift from the index; compare 2023 with 2024 to estimate how strongly nursing-home supply responds to the rate, the elasticity the comparison with a rate increase needs; work out each hospital's share of the saving, which sets its share of the cost; and show nursing-home cost per day against the Medicaid rates from 2011 to 2026 as the evidence figure.

The strongest objection I expect is that the rate rule is itself a statewide rate increase. Rebasing or indexing raises the rate on all 526,866 Medicaid days over time, and part of any increase goes to homes already above cost rather than to new beds. So the analysis prices the rule on its own and compares it head to head with a one-time statewide increase and with the partnership, per bed each actually adds.
